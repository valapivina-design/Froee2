The routes compared

Attorney, class action, small claims, recovery service

Attorney (contingency or hourly). Expect retainers or 25–40% contingency on recovery; strong when the defendant has assets and a jurisdiction; slow — 12–36 months is normal.

Class action. Low cost per victim and real pressure on larger operators, but payouts arrive years later and only if the case survives certification; you lose control of the file.

Small claims court. Filing fees in the low hundreds, no lawyers required, hearings in weeks: the default route against identified payment beneficiaries.

Recovery service. Not litigation but evidence work — tracing, dispute packaging, regulator filings — priced per case; the fastest route when the money trail is still warm.

Cost transparency

The numbers behind each option

  • Attorney hourly rates.Financial-fraud specialists typically run several hundred per hour; even a 'simple' letter campaign can cost four figures before any filing.
  • Contingency percentages.25–40% of recovered sums is standard; ask who fronts the filing fees and expert costs.
  • Small claims limits.Caps vary by jurisdiction (often up to five figures) — enough for many individual losses against a reachable defendant.
  • Free levers first.Chargebacks, bank recalls and regulator complaints cost nothing and regularly outperform paid routes when done properly.
The decision framework

Choose the route to match the target

Trace before you spend

A day's tracing tells you whether a solvent, reachable defendant exists — the answer decides between small claims, attorney letters or pure dispute work.

Spend proportionally

Legal fees should scale with realistic recovery; a five-figure case rarely justifies six-figure litigation.

Combine routes in sequence

Chargeback first, recall second, small claims third, attorney last — each stage is cheaper than the one before it and often makes the next unnecessary.

If the card or bank route is still open, the chargeback guide and the wire guide cover the free levers; the deadline guide protects the windows those routes depend on.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to sue a broker?

It depends on the route: small claims runs to low hundreds in filing fees with no lawyer; attorneys typically charge retainers or 25–40% contingency, with cases taking one to three years; class actions cost little upfront but pay out late, if at all. The honest answer starts with tracing: who you can actually reach decides what any route is worth.

Is small claims court viable for investment fraud?

Often yes — against the payment beneficiary, not the offshore shell. Filing is cheap, hearings are fast, and a judgement against the account holder who received your wire is enforceable where the shell's never was.

Class action against a broker — worth joining?

Join if a coordinated action against a larger operator exists and you are patient; the costs are low and the pressure real. Do not wait for one instead of acting: individual dispute windows close while class actions take years to certify.
