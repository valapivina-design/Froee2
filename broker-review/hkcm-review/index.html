The short answer: HKCM is real — but read this before you hand over any money

HKCM is a German subscription chart-analysis service (Stuttgart-based; Elliott Wave and Fibonacci coverage of stocks, indices, crypto and commodities) run by the Hoss & Hopf YouTube/TikTok personalities — an information product, not a broker, and not a Hong Kong capital-markets firm These are the facts that check out on public registers — the complaint record is what our review is still working through:

  • A real corporate presence: Hasenbergsteige 5, 70178 Stuttgart, a proper German Impressum, named public-facing analysts (Philip Hopf, Philip Klinkmüller), daily YouTube output and dated editorial content (HKCM Insights, Oct 2026) — the opposite of a fake-broker template. It even runs its own “Antiscam” page.
  • The model is custody-free: it sells information subscriptions; no client funds are taken beyond subscription fees, so there is no withdrawal that could be blocked.

Checking out is not the same as closed: the complaints against HKCM are under active investigation, and none of the facts above protects you from a fake. Most 'hkcm scam' searches trace to impersonators, not the platform itself — the ones that don't are the reason we investigate.

One more thing before you deposit: the complaints around HKCM are under investigation — by us, not by a regulator. The honest caveats. Marketing claims an “over 70% hit rate” (Traden.de forum, from 22 Mar 2024): subscribers praise its BTC/ETH analysis, but one user reports following a public Canopy Growth call and losing ~90%, and forum sceptics read the public wins as “lucky coincidences” rather than a reproducible system. Per-product pricing up to €1,000+/year is steep for an unverifiable track record, and Trustpilot shows 3.2/5 from a single review — a near-zero footprint unusual for a claimed large subscriber base. The risk is subscription value, not deposit safety.

Sort your situation

Real-platform dispute or impersonation fraud?

Both feel identical from the inside. They are fixed by completely different routes — and the fastest way to tell them apart is to ask three questions.

If it is the real platform: your dispute runs through its formal complaints process, then the regulator or ombudsman. Slow, but it usually works. Size any subscription against the fact that no reproducible, audited track record is published — treat the “70% hit rate” as marketing until proven otherwise. And disambiguate: HKCM here is the Stuttgart analysis service; complaints about “Hong Kong capital management” namesakes are different entities entirely.

If it was an impersonator: you were never a customer of HKCM at all. A clone site, a fake “account manager”, a lookalike domain — the money went to criminals, not to the platform. That is fraud, and speed decides what can be recovered.

The three questions: did you type the official domain yourself, or follow a link, advert or message? Did anyone contact you first? Were you asked to pay a personal account or a different domain? If any answer is wrong, treat it as impersonation — and start here.
