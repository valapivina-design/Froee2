The short answer

CEX.IO is a real exchange — “CEX Capital ATS” is not CEX.IO

First, the good news — the real brand checks out:

  • CEX.IO (cex.io) is a genuine, long-operating cryptocurrency exchange with a verifiable corporate history since 2013.
  • The impersonation value is the trust earned by a decade of operation — the exact thing a 14-month-old phishing-scored domain does not have.

Now the bad news — the clones:

  • Gridinsoft (17 Jun 2026): phishing indicators, trust score 1/100, domain about 14 months old.
  • ScamAdviser: very low trust score.
  • Straja: serious financial-fraud risk with phishing indicators.
  • Trustpilot carries its “received regulatory attention” banner; 2.8/5 from three reviews.

If the site you used is not the official domain — cex.io — it was not CEX Capital ATS. It was someone wearing its name.

Sort your situation

Real-platform dispute or impersonation fraud?

Both feel identical from the inside. They are fixed by completely different routes — and the fastest way to tell them apart is to ask three questions.

If it is the real platform: your dispute runs through its formal complaints process, then the regulator or ombudsman. Slow, but it usually works. Type cex.io yourself and distrust every lookalike. The “ATS” suffix is decoration — alternative trading systems are a US regulatory category, not something a crypto clone acquires by naming.

If it was an impersonator: you were never a customer of CEX Capital ATS at all. A clone site, a fake “account manager”, a lookalike domain — the money went to criminals, not to the platform. That is fraud, and speed decides what can be recovered.

The three questions: did you type the official domain yourself, or follow a link, advert or message? Did anyone contact you first? Were you asked to pay a personal account or a different domain? If any answer is wrong, treat it as impersonation — and start here.

Frequently asked questions

Is CEX Capital ATS a scam?

The real CEX.IO, no — it is a legitimate long-running exchange at cex.io. The site at cexcapital-ats.com, yes: it borrows the CEX name with an institutional-sounding suffix, sits on a young domain, and scores 1/100 on phishing scanners. If you entered credentials or deposited there, you dealt with an impersonator, not with the exchange.

How do I check the site I used was genuine?

Type the official domain yourself — cex.io — never follow a link from an ad, email or message. Then check the regulator's public register, which lists the firm's genuine website. If your domain differs by even one character, it was a fake.
